Jalaj Verma was nineteen when he started reading skincare comment sections the way
other people read the news. Not the posts — the comments. Hundreds of them a night,
on other brands' advertisements, screenshotting the ones that sounded confused.

## The comment section as research

There is a particular kind of question that keeps appearing under skincare ads in
India, and it is almost never about the product being advertised. It is about the
five products the person is already using, in what order, and whether any of them
are cancelling each other out.

"Nobody was answering those," he says. "Everyone was selling."

The answers he started writing were long, unpaid, and slightly obsessive. They were
also, in retrospect, the entire product strategy.

### What the DMs actually said

Three patterns showed up again and again:

1. **Too many steps.** People had been sold routines, not products, and the routines
   had grown to seven or eight items.
2. **No sequencing.** Nobody knew what went on first, and the packaging never said.
3. **Ingredient anxiety.** Everyone had heard of niacinamide. Almost nobody could say
   what it did.

> We did not set out to build a simple brand because simple was a nice aesthetic. We
> built it simple because the complexity was the thing making people miserable.

## Three friends and a spreadsheet

The founding team came out of the same Jhansi school year — a chemist, a designer,
and someone who could actually talk to manufacturers without getting quoted a
first-timer's price. The first formulation ran to fourteen revisions.

What did not change across those fourteen revisions was the count: *four products.*
Not a range. Not a system. Four.

## What it cost

The first batch sold out in eleven days, which sounds like a triumph and was in fact
a small disaster — they had no second batch, no supplier slack, and a comment section
that had just been taught to expect answers within the hour.
